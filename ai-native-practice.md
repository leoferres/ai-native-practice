# AI-Native Inquiry: A Statement of Practice

Artificial Phenomenology is an AI-native project.

I use AI throughout the work: to develop questions, explore ideas, challenge arguments, find connections, write code, organize material, and produce and revise prose. Readers should assume that AI has been involved in anything published as part of this project.

I do not regard that involvement as a defect requiring an apology. Nor do I believe that the important question is whether a particular sentence was written by a human or generated by a machine. The important questions are what the work claims, what supports those claims, whether the reasoning withstands criticism, and whether the result is of any use.

If I publish something under my name, I am responsible for it. I have decided that it represents what I want to say. I am answerable for its errors, regardless of whether those errors originated with me or with a system I used. AI does not get to be the author when the work succeeds and the culprit when it fails.

I will not document every prompt or assign artificial percentages of human and machine authorship to every text. I will, however, explain the role of AI when it affects the evidential or methodological status of the work. This includes, for example, AI-generated examples that might be mistaken for observations, analysis code produced by a model, AI participation in interpreting data, or accounts of sources that I have not independently read in full.

I will verify consequential claims, quotations, citations, and sources. I will not pretend to have read something I have not read. I will respect privacy, confidentiality, and consent. When I find an error, I will correct it publicly rather than invoke the unreliability of AI as an excuse.

I also undertake not to manufacture content merely because content has become cheap to produce. AI makes it possible to generate more text than anyone should reasonably ask others to read. I will decide what is worth pursuing and publishing. The relevant human contribution is not keystrokes or exhaustion, but judgment: choosing questions, rejecting plausible nonsense, testing claims, recognizing failure, and accepting responsibility for the result.

This is an experiment, and its outcome is not predetermined. Close work with AI may extend my thinking, but it may also flatten it, shape its vocabulary, or make fluency look like understanding. I intend to examine those effects as part of the project, including failures and cases in which the system has led the inquiry astray.

Artificial Phenomenology is not yet a conventional scientific program, and I will not manufacture academic authority for it by presenting it as one. Its principal venue will be the open internet. That does not mean that evidence, criticism, scholarly methods, or academic venues are irrelevant. I will use them when they serve the inquiry, without treating institutional approval as the measure of its value.

The experiment is not whether AI can produce more words. We already know that it can. The experiment is whether sustained collaboration with AI can help produce forms of thought and inquiry that would otherwise not exist.
